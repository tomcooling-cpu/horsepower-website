I have been prescribing cadence work since the day I started coaching, and I still have not found anything that gives a runner more for less. To my mind it is the number one lever for making someone faster, keeping them out of the physio's waiting room, and stopping their form falling apart in the last hour of a race. That is my coaching view, and I will be straight with you about where the research backs it up and where it is simply what I see. But if you take one thing from this post, take this: quicken your step.

## What does cadence actually change?

Cadence is just the number of steps you take per minute. Change it at a fixed speed and the stride has to change with it, because speed is cadence multiplied by stride length. Quicken the step and three things happen. The foot lands closer to underneath you, you bounce less, and you brake less.

This has been measured properly. When 45 recreational runners ran at the same speed with their cadence raised 5 and 10 per cent above their natural rate, step length shortened, the up and down travel of their centre of mass dropped, braking impulse dropped, and the energy absorbed at the knee fell, with the hip joining in at 10 per cent. Drop the cadence 10 per cent and every joint absorbed substantially more (Heiderscheit et al., 2011). A systematic review of ten such studies found the same picture: a quicker step means less vertical movement, lower ground reaction force, less shock, and less energy absorbed at the hip, knee and ankle (Schubert, Kempf and Heiderscheit, 2014), and it cuts peak loading on the glutes during landing too (Lenhart, Thelen and Heiderscheit, 2014).

That is the mechanics behind what I tell athletes in plainer words: better cadence means lower vertical oscillation, less braking force on every step, and less force going up through the body. The same effort goes forward instead of up and down.

## Why do bouncing and braking cost you?

Two reasons: economy and injury.

Lower vertical oscillation is one of the technique factors most consistently linked with better running economy (Moore, 2016). In the biggest study of its kind, 97 runners of all standards were filmed on a treadmill, and three measures, how much the pelvis moved up and down during ground contact, how much the knee bent, and how much the runner slowed on landing, between them explained 39 per cent of the variation in energy cost. Braking was also one of the variables tied to season's best time (Folland et al., 2017). Every time the foot lands out in front of you, you hit the brakes and then pay to get back up to speed. Where the limb is at the moment of contact shapes the loading that follows (Wille et al., 2014), and a heel striker in shoes generates a markedly larger collision force than a barefoot forefoot striker (Lieberman et al., 2010).

Then there is injury. Over a season of high school cross country, runners in the lowest cadence group, 164 steps per minute or fewer, were more than six times as likely to pick up a shin injury as those at 174 and above (Luedke et al., 2016). In runners with knee pain, a single session with a metronome lifting step rate by 10 per cent improved their mechanics, pain and function, and they were running further pain free three months later (Bramah et al., 2019). One session and a cheap app. Not many interventions in running pay back like that.

I do need to be honest on one point. The lab has not crowned cadence the king of running speed. Moore's review notes that most runners naturally settle on a stride length close to their most economical, with only small deviations, up to around 3 per cent shorter, doing any good. So if you are already efficient, a big cadence change will not make you instantly faster. What I see in coaching is that very few amateurs are already efficient. The runners who come to me at 155 to 165 have a lot of room, and the reduction in load arrives in week one whether or not the economy numbers move straight away.

## What happens when you get tired?

This is where cadence earns its keep, and where my view was formed, standing at the roadside in the back half of marathons, Ironmans and ultras.

When fatigue arrives, cadence drifts down. In 179 runners filmed at 8 km and again at 40 km of a marathon, everyone slowed, cadence fell, ground contact time went up by 13 per cent, and the knee bent less during the support phase (Chan-Roper et al., 2012). Even experienced runners on a hard hour on the treadmill saw their natural cadence slip (Hunter and Smith, 2007). At the very top, filming the World Championship marathons showed step length and cadence both falling between 29.5 km and 40 km, and the authors concluded that training the fatigue resistance of the key muscles and tendons, so that cadence and stride hold up late, matters more than which part of the foot you land on (Hanley, Bissas and Merlino, 2020).

Here is the pattern I see, and this is my observation rather than a published finding. The lower your cadence is to begin with, the steeper that late-race decline tends to be. If you start a marathon at 155 to 160 and fade to 140, you are effectively sitting down in your run form. The hips drop, the foot reaches out, every landing is a brake, and your body is fighting forward movement with every step. If you start at 180 and fade to 175, you are still running well. Same fatigue, completely different outcome, because you had somewhere to fall from.

Interestingly, the best ultra runners appear to do something different, and I think it proves the same point from the other direction. Across a 100 km World Championship road race, elite runners averaged 182 steps per minute, and once you allowed for speed their cadence did not drop with fatigue at all (Burns, Zendler and Zernicke, 2019). Over a 24 hour treadmill run, experienced ultra runners actually quickened their step by around 5 per cent and landed with lower peak forces, which the authors read as the body protecting itself (Morin, Samozino and Millet, 2011). The people who can run for a day keep the step short and quick when everything else is failing. The rest of us have to train that in on purpose.

## Is 180 the magic number?

The number comes from Jack Daniels, who counted the steps of the runners at the 1984 Olympics, from the 800 m to the marathon, and found that almost all of them were at 180 or more (Daniels, 2014). Notice the "or more". It was a floor for elite runners at race pace, not a prescription, and the elite 100 km runners above ranged from 155 to 203, with taller runners sitting naturally lower (Burns, Zendler and Zernicke, 2019).

So I treat 180 as a target, not a law. What I ask my athletes to do is lock in 180 steps per minute at mid to high zone 2, the easy aerobic running that makes up most of their week. Do that, and you will likely see 185 to 195 at tempo and in efforts without trying, because cadence rises with speed, by roughly 5 to 6 steps per minute for every metre per second of extra pace in the 100 km data (Burns, Zendler and Zernicke, 2019). That is the point. With a quick step as your base, going faster means applying a bit more force, pushing off a bit harder, or both. Without it, going faster means reaching the leg out and lengthening the stride, which turns running into a strength activity: overstriding, heel striking, a jolt through the foot, stress through the body, and a brake on every step.

## Why not just buy a more supportive shoe?

Because if you focus on strength, you do not need the motion control shoe. The foot does that job for you. That is the whole of my argument, and the rest of this section is why I believe it.

A motion control shoe exists to stop the arch collapsing inwards under load. But you already own something built for exactly that. Tibialis posterior, the muscle that runs down the inside of the shin and under the arch, is the primary dynamic stabiliser of the medial arch in gait (Semple et al., 2009). Underneath it sits the foot core, a system of small intrinsic muscles that control the arch the way the deep trunk muscles control the spine, and the people who proposed that model made the point that treatment for foot problems usually goes straight to supporting the foot from the outside rather than training those muscles to work as designed (McKeon et al., 2015). Above both sits the calf, taking the landing and driving the push off. Strong and working together, that system controls pronation and absorbs load from the inside. A shoe does it from the outside, and a foot that has its job done for it never gets any stronger.

Train the system and it changes, measurably. Four weeks of the short foot exercise, which is simply learning to draw the arch up without curling the toes, reduced navicular drop, the standard measure of how far the arch collapses under weight, by around 2 mm and improved single leg balance (Mulligan and Cook, 2013). That is the foot doing what the shoe was sold to do. In a year long randomised trial, recreational runners who followed an eight week foot and ankle strengthening programme and kept it going had a 2.4 fold lower rate of running injuries than the control group (Taddei et al., 2020). For tibialis posterior itself, progressive resistance work improved pain and function in early tendon problems beyond what orthoses alone managed (Kulig et al., 2009).

Now the honest part. The research does say that motion control shoes can help a pronated runner. In the one large blinded trial on the subject, 372 recreational runners were given a standard or a motion control version of the same shoe, and the motion control group had fewer injuries over six months, with the benefit concentrated in runners with pronated feet (Malisoux et al., 2016). I am not going to pretend that study does not exist, and if you are injured right now I am not telling you to throw your shoes away. The wider evidence for prescribing shoes by foot type is thin, though. A review that went looking for trials to support it found none (Richards, Magin and Callister, 2009), a trial that randomised women runners to neutral, stability or motion control shoes found the motion control shoe produced more pain (Ryan et al., 2011), and the leading name in footwear research has argued that impact and pronation were never the injury predictors the industry assumed (Nigg et al., 2015).

So my position is a coaching choice, and I will own it as one. The shoe can help in the short term, but it does nothing to make you stronger. I would rather build the foot that does not need it, and in my experience, once an athlete has done a few months of the strength work, they stop asking which shoe will fix them. One warning, because I have watched people get this wrong: do not run in minimal shoes overnight. When experienced runners switched to barefoot style shoes over ten weeks, more than half showed new bone stress in the feet on MRI (Ridge et al., 2013). Build the foot first, then let the shoe get out of the way.

## How do I build cadence?

Patiently. The body will adapt to a quicker step, but tendons and calves need weeks, not days.

- **Measure first.** Most watches show cadence. Look at a few easy runs and note your natural number. Count both feet; some devices still report one.
- **Go up in steps of 5 to 10 per cent, never more.** That is what the lab used (Heiderscheit et al., 2011), and it is enough. From 160, aim for 168 to 172 first, hold it for two to three weeks until it feels normal, then go again. The goal is 180 at mid to high zone 2.
- **Use a cue.** A metronome app, a playlist at the right tempo, or a cadence alert on the watch. The knee pain study used a metronome and a watch alert, nothing fancier (Bramah et al., 2019).
- **Think short and quick, not fast.** Shorter stride, feet landing under the hips, light and quiet. Your pace should not change on an easy run; if it does, you have sped up rather than quickened.
- **At tempo and in efforts, let it rise.** 185 to 195 is normal once 180 is your easy day baseline. If your hard running sits at 175, that is the overstriding I talked about above.
- **Hills are the test.** Keep the step quick going up, shorten it going down. Uphill is where cadence usually goes first, and downhill is where braking does the damage.

## What strength work goes with it?

Cadence protects you from load, but you still need a lower leg that can take what is left. I target the tibialis posterior, the calf and the foot itself, because between them they hold the arch, control the landing and drive the push off.

- **Tibialis posterior.** The muscle I see weak most often in runners who have lived in supportive shoes. The exercise that hits it most selectively is resisted foot adduction: sat down, band around the forefoot anchored to the outside, draw the foot inwards against the band, slowly back out, two to three sets of 15 to 20 (Kulig et al., 2004).
- **Calf.** Single leg heel raises, straight knee and bent knee, slow on the way down, building to loaded. Straight knee works the gastrocnemius, bent knee the soleus, and the heel raise recruits tibialis posterior as well (Kulig et al., 2004). Three sets to a hard 12 to 15, twice a week.
- **Foot core.** The short foot exercise: standing, draw the ball of the big toe back towards the heel to lift the arch without curling the toes, hold five seconds, repeat (McKeon et al., 2015; Mulligan and Cook, 2013). Add toe spreading, towel scrunches and single leg balance barefoot.
- **Walk in less shoe.** Minimal shoes for walking, step count built up week by week, grew foot muscle size and strength as much as a formal exercise programme (Ridge et al., 2019). Walking, not running, until the foot has earned it.
- **Hop, skip, bound.** Short, low volume drills, once the strength is there, teach the foot and calf to be springy at contact. Little and often, never on a tired leg.

None of this needs a gym. Ten minutes, three times a week, done consistently for a few months, is the dose.

## The short version

Quicken your step. Get to 180 at easy pace in steps of 5 to 10 per cent, with a metronome or a watch alert, and let it rise naturally when you run hard. You will bounce less, brake less, and put less through your knees and hips on every stride, and when the fatigue arrives late in the race you will have somewhere to fade from. Strengthen the tibialis posterior, the calf and the foot so the leg can carry the load, and let the foot do what it was designed to do before you pay a shoe to do it instead.
