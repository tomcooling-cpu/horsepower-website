Don't Get Fancy Before You're Fancy

By Tom Cooling · 7 August 2026

Most athletes reach for the fancy stuff long before they've earned it. New wheels, a heat protocol, ketones, some exotic interval format off the internet, all while their easy days are a mess and they've not done a single session that looks like their actual race. So here's the blunt version I give my athletes: don't get fancy before you're fancy. Nail the basics first, because that's where nearly all of your improvement is sitting, unclaimed.

What is the low-hanging fruit?

It's the boring stuff that works. For almost everyone reading this, the biggest gains are still sitting in three places, and none of them cost a penny.

  • Consistency. The single most powerful thing in endurance training is turning up, week after week, without the big gaps and the boom-bust cycles. Twelve consistent weeks beats four brilliant weeks and eight lost ones every single time. If your training history has holes in it, that's your first and biggest gain, and no gadget touches it.
  • Specificity. Your training should look like your event. If you're racing a hilly long-course triathlon, your key sessions should rehearse hills, duration and race intensity, not generic blocks that could belong to anyone. Train the demands you're actually going to face.
  • Your biggest limiter. Everyone has one thing holding them back more than the rest. Fix that first. Spending your effort polishing a strength while your limiter sits there untouched is the most common way I see people train hard and improve slowly.

Get those three right and you'll beat the athlete with the better kit and the worse habits nearly every time.

Why do the basics get ignored?

Because they're not exciting, and because the fancy stuff is where the marketing lives. Nobody sells you consistency. There's no product for turning up. The industry makes its money on the one per cent gains, so the one per cent gains are what fill your feed, and it's easy to convince yourself the reason you're not improving is a missing supplement or a smarter interval structure, rather than the eight weeks you took off over winter.

There's an ego cost to the basics too. Riding genuinely easy on your easy days feels like you're not trying. Doing the same specific race-simulation session again feels less clever than inventing a new one. Facing your biggest limiter means spending your training time on the thing you're worst at, in front of everyone, which is uncomfortable. All of that pushes people towards the shiny, complicated work and away from the plain work that actually moves the needle. Honestly, a big part of my job is just giving people permission to keep doing the boring thing that's working.

How do I find my biggest limiter?

Look at where the race actually falls apart for you, not where it feels hardest in the moment. If you fade badly in the last third of every event, your limiter is probably durability or fuelling, not top-end power. If you get shelled on the first steep climb, it might be your VO2 ceiling. If your easy pace and your race pace are almost the same number, your aerobic base is thin and that's the whole job.

Be honest here, because the limiter is usually the thing you least enjoy training. That's precisely why it stayed a limiter.

The other honest test is to look at your training, not your intentions. Pull up the last couple of months and ask what you actually did, not what the plan said. If ninety per cent of your rides look the same, medium-hard, medium-length, no real easy and no real hard, then variety and structure are your limiter before anything physiological. If you never once trained the specific demand of your event, specificity is your limiter. The data usually tells you plainly what you've been avoiding.

What does specificity actually look like?

Specificity isn't a slogan, it's a set of concrete choices. Take a hilly long-course triathlon as the example. Specific training means your long ride isn't a flat aerobic cruise the whole way through; in the final third it carries the climbs and the race-effort blocks you'll actually meet on the day, held at the intensity you intend to race, with the fuelling you intend to use. It means your long run rehearses running on tired legs, at the pace and duration the race demands, not a comfortable trot on fresh ones. It means your key bike session confirms the wattage you can genuinely hold for the real duration, so your race plan is built on numbers you've actually produced rather than hoped for.

Generic training builds generic fitness. It's not that a random hard ride does nothing; it's that it does less, per hour, than the same hour spent rehearsing the thing you're trying to be good at. The closer your training looks to your event, the more of your fitness shows up on race day instead of staying in the gym.

When am I allowed the fancy stuff?

When the basics are genuinely handled, not when you've decided you're bored of them. The advanced work and the marginal gains are real, but they're marginal by definition. A one per cent gain from some clever intervention is worth chasing once you've already banked the twenty per cent sitting in consistency, specificity and your limiter. Chase it before that and you're decorating a house with no foundations.

There's an order to it, and the order isn't negotiable:

  1. Show up consistently for long enough to build a base.
  2. Make your key sessions specific to your event.
  3. Find your biggest limiter and put real work into it.
  4. Only then start layering in the refinements that turn good into slightly better.

The short version

You don't need the fancy stuff until you've earned it, and most people haven't. The unglamorous work, turning up, training the actual demands of your race, and hammering your weakest link, is where the improvement lives. Do that properly for a season and you'll be amazed how far it takes you before you need a single marginal gain. Get fancy after you're fancy, not before.
